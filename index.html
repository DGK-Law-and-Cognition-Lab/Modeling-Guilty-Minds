Inferring Criminal Mental States from Behavioral Evidence

Laypeople distinguish Model Penal Code categories but treat knowledge as qualitatively distinct
David G. Kamper, Andrew Bistras, Keith J. Holyoak, & Jaime Castrellon
Department of Psychology, University of California, Los Angeles

Abstract

The Model Penal Code (MPC) distinguishes four levels of criminal culpability (purpose, knowledge, recklessness, and negligence), yet whether laypeople can reliably make these distinctions through genuine inference remains unclear. Prior studies either presented participants with explicitly labeled mental states or asked for self-assessed judgments under artificial precision. We addressed this gap by requiring 212 participants to infer a protagonist's mental state from behavioral evidence in contraband smuggling vignettes with six systematically varied binary features. Because the scenario involved a protagonist recruited to carry a package rather than one who initiated the act, purpose attributions were not supported; participants classified the protagonist as blameless, negligent, reckless, or knowing. Cumulative link mixed models showed that participants' risk assessments strongly predicted their categorizations (b = 0.046, z = 14.87, p < .001), and that payment was the only vignette feature to independently shift judgments toward higher culpability after correction for multiple comparisons (b = 0.53, z = 3.91, p < .001). Critically, knowledge judgments were associated with lower risk ratings than recklessness judgments (d = −0.28, p = .002), and the proportional odds assumption was violated, with the risk coefficient at the knowledge threshold 3.2 times weaker than at lower thresholds. A features-only Random Forest with SHAP analysis revealed that knowledge was the only category where the type of contraband rather than payment dominated feature importance. Representational similarity analysis confirmed that semantic distance between vignettes predicted judgment distance (ρ = 0.077, p < .001), but only when using an embedding model suited to detecting small word-level changes in templated texts. These results indicate that laypeople can systematically distinguish MPC mental states through inferential reasoning, but that knowledge functions as a qualitatively distinct category driven by categorical inference rather than extreme risk perception.
